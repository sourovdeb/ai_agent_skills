## Structure every substantive analysis as CREAC, not bare IRAC

Conclusion first, then Rule, then a Rule Explanation that actually synthesizes the relevant authority (not just cites it), then Application to the specific facts, then a restated Conclusion. The Rule Explanation step is what separates rigorous analysis from a formulaic template — it's where you show *why* the rule is what it is, reconciling cases that seem to conflict, noting splits, and explaining which line of authority actually governs here.

- **Conclusion**: the bottom-line answer, stated plainly, up front.
- **Rule**: the governing legal standard, cited to its actual source (statute, regulation, controlling case).
- **Rule Explanation**: how courts or regulators have actually applied and refined that rule — this is the substantive work, not a formality.
- **Application**: the rule and its explanation applied to these specific facts, addressing counterarguments honestly rather than only the favorable reading.
- **Conclusion**: restated, now earned rather than asserted.

## Frame jurisdiction explicitly, every time

Name the governing jurisdiction in the question presented itself ("Under [state/federal/EU] law, does...") rather than leaving it implicit — a huge share of legal errors come from silently assuming the wrong jurisdiction's rule applies. Keep choice-of-law (which jurisdiction's substantive rule governs) distinct from choice-of-forum (where a matter would be filed) — these are different questions and conflating them produces analysis that answers the wrong one.

For multi-jurisdiction dossiers specifically: build a jurisdiction matrix as the first section — which jurisdictions are actually implicated, what's materially different about each one's rule on the issue at hand, and where the analysis is genuinely uniform versus where it forks. Don't produce one analysis and footnote "this may vary by state" — that's exactly the caveat a Harvard-trained associate would be expected to actually resolve, not wave at.

## Cite with the discipline of real signal weight

Use citation signals to communicate exactly how much support a source provides, the way Bluebook practice does:
- No signal: the cited authority directly states the proposition.
- *See*: an inferential step is needed to get from the authority to the proposition.
- *See also*: additional support, cumulative to a preceding citation.
- *Cf.*: the authority is analogous, not directly on point.
- *But see* / *but cf.*: contrary or complicating authority — flagging this rather than omitting it is what makes analysis trustworthy rather than advocacy dressed as neutral analysis.
- Note the authority's weight explicitly: binding precedent in the relevant jurisdiction, persuasive-only from another jurisdiction, or dicta — don't let a reader assume binding weight that isn't there.

## Garner's mechanical edit pass

Before finalizing any written work product, run a pass that specifically:
- Replaces weak "to be" constructions (is, are, was, were) with an active verb wherever it strengthens the sentence without losing precision.
- Cuts throat-clearing ("It is important to note that...", "It should be noted that...") — state the point directly.
- Prefers the plain word over the ornate one where legal precision doesn't require the ornate one ("use" over "utilize," "before" over "prior to").
Precision beats plainness only where the technical term is actually doing legal work (e.g., "shall" vs. "may" in a drafted provision) — don't sacrifice a term of art for the sake of plain language when the term of art is load-bearing.

## Regulatory/compliance dossiers: score risk explicitly

For compliance-flavored work, don't just list obligations — score them. A standard, defensible approach: rate Likelihood (1-5) and Impact (1-5) for each identified risk or gap, combine into a Low/Medium/High/Critical band, and tie a review cadence to the band (Critical = immediate remediation and re-review; Low = next scheduled audit cycle). This turns a wall of regulatory text into something a client can actually act on and prioritize.

## What to flag rather than assert

- Genuinely unsettled law (a live circuit split, a statute with no controlling interpretation yet) — say it's unsettled, give the competing readings, don't manufacture false confidence in one answer.
- Anything that depends on facts not yet established — name what fact would change the analysis, rather than assuming a favorable fact pattern.
- Any point where the practical answer is "this needs to be confirmed with local counsel" — particularly for anything cross-border, licensing-specific, or procedurally technical (filing deadlines, service rules) where a wrong assumption is costly and jurisdiction-specific detail changes fast.
