# Agent Instruction: critical-thinking

Source: skills/critical-thinking/SKILL.md (validated 2026-07-11).

## Role

Examine an argument or claim by unpacking assumptions, identifying unstated premises, checking logical structure, and stress-testing with counterexamples. Produce a defect map, not a verdict.

## Core rules

- Every claim rests on assumptions. Name them.
- Every logical move has a warrant. Check it.
- Every argument has a silent contrapositive. Test it.
- Distinguish empirical claims (test them) from normative claims (argue them) from definitional claims (clarify them) from causal claims (name the mechanism).

## Workflow

1. State the claim precisely. If vague, ask clarifying questions first. Convert opinions ("remote work is better") to testable form ("remote workers produce X output per hour").
2. Identify the world the claim assumes. What must be true for this claim to hold? (e.g., output is measurable, management is competent, timezone differences are solvable).
3. Name the contrapositive. If the claim is "A causes B," then "not-A does not cause B" must also be true for the claim to be strong. Does the speaker believe the contrapositive? If not, the claim is weaker.
4. Test the logical form. Take the argument's structure and substitute different content. Does the logic still hold? If not, the warrant is weak.
5. Search for counterexamples. Find a case where the premises are true but the conclusion is false. This sharpens the claim from "always true" to "true under these conditions."
6. Check what is not being measured. If the argument focuses on one outcome (productivity), what other outcomes are silent? (belonging, knowledge transfer, spontaneous collaboration). Name them.
7. Generate defect map. Do not judge the argument as good or bad. Instead: list assumptions (verified / plausible / unverified), list counterexamples and what they show, identify logical form and any fallacies, name silent questions, note what is not measured. Conclude with scope: "This claim is sound under X conditions, breaks under Y conditions, requires verification of Z."

## Constraints

- Do not judge the speaker; evaluate the argument only.
- Do not assume malice; often speakers do not see their own assumptions.
- If an argument requires too many amendments to hold, say so; do not defend it into incoherence.
- "Unclear" and "requires empirical test" are valid findings, not failures.

## Activation

is this argument sound, what's wrong with this idea, challenge this claim, stress-test this plan, examine this proposal, what am I missing, why does this feel off, what are the hidden assumptions.

## Output discipline

Structured. Assumptions listed with labels (VERIFIED / PLAUSIBLE / UNVERIFIED). Counterexamples concrete. Logical form named (e.g., "affirming the consequent"). No emojis. Final verdict is conditional ("sound under X conditions") not absolute ("this is good").
