# Skill: Critical Thinking

Examine an argument or claim by unpacking assumptions, identifying unstated premises, checking logical structure, and stress-testing with counterexamples. The output is a defect map, not a verdict.

## Core rule

Every claim rests on assumptions. Name them. Every logical move has a warrant. Check it. Every argument has a silent contrapositive. Test it.

## Rule: Unpack before judging

"That's a good idea" is not an examination; it's a reaction. Unpack: What is the claim exactly (not what it sounds like)? What would the world need to look like for it to be true? What world would make it false? Until you can answer these three questions precisely, do not evaluate.

## Rule: Surface the assumptions

"Remote work increases productivity" assumes (1) productivity can be measured, (2) the measurement method is valid, (3) other factors are controlled, (4) the causation runs from location to output (not reverse—high-output people choose remote work). Do not assume the speaker knows these assumptions; often they do not. Naming them is not attack; it is clarity.

## Rule: Check the logical structure

Does the argument assume what it is trying to prove (begging the question)? Does it claim "all X have property Y" then cite one Y to prove X exists (affirming the consequent)? Does it say "if not our solution, then chaos" (false dilemma)? Name the logical form, not the politics.

## Rule: Stress-test with counterexamples

A claim "people leave jobs because of bad management" survives the counterexample "some people leave for higher pay even with good management"? Then the claim is too broad; refine it. "People leave jobs for ONE of: bad management, pay mismatch, better opportunity, burnout" is testable. Counterexamples do not kill arguments; they sharpen them.

## Rule: Name the silent question

Every argument suppresses a question. "We should automate this task because it takes 40 hours per month" suppresses "is the automation cost less than the labor cost?" and "what happens to the person doing those 40 hours?" Name what is not being asked.

## Rule: Distinguish claim types

EMPIRICAL (measurable in principle; test it): "Remote workers produce 15% more code." NORMATIVE (value judgment; argument, not test): "We should value output over presence." DEFINITIONAL (word choice; clarify it): "Productivity means output or output-per-hour-worked?" CAUSAL (mechanism; hard to prove): "Remote work causes productivity gains (maybe selection, maybe focus, maybe fewer meetings)." A single argument often mixes types and pretends they are all empirical.

## Workflow: Unpack (Steps 1–3)

**Step 1: State the claim precisely.** Not "remote work is better" but "remote workers produce more code per hour than office workers, controlling for role and experience." If the original is vague, ask clarifying questions.

**Step 2: Identify the world it assumes.** Remote work claims assume: people can focus without an office, management can measure output, meetings are not necessary for collaboration, timezone differences are solvable, home environment is adequate. Which assumptions hold? Which don't?

**Step 3: Name the contrapositive.** If "remote work increases productivity," then "office work must decrease it." Is that claim as strong as the forward claim? Does the speaker believe it? If not, the original is weaker than stated.

## Workflow: Stress-test (Steps 4–6)

**Step 4: Test the logical form.** Take the argument's structure and plug in a different topic. "Tall people are successful because tall people are more visible in meetings." Plug in "bald people." Does the logic still work? If the form fails with different content, the warrant is weak.

**Step 5: Search for counterexamples.** Find a case where the premise is true but the conclusion false. Remote work + high productivity? Yes, many cases. Remote work + low productivity? Yes, some cases (isolation, distraction, loss of culture). This does not kill the claim; it shows the claim is conditional. What conditions matter?

**Step 6: Check what is not being measured.** If the argument is "remote work increases productivity," it probably does not measure "sense of belonging," "knowledge transfer speed," or "spontaneous collaboration." These might matter. Name them.

## Workflow: Verdict (Step 7)

**Step 7: Defect map, not judgment.** Do not conclude "the argument is good" or "it's bad." Instead: "The claim rests on three assumptions, all plausible; one (management can measure output reliably) is unverified; one counterexample exists (isolation correlates with lower productivity in some roles); the logical form is sound; three silent questions are not addressed (cost of automation, impact on displaced workers, timezone effects). Under these conditions, the claim is conditional and limited in scope."

## Safety gates

- Do not judge the speaker (ad hominem is a fallacy; evaluate the argument).
- Do not assume malice (often speakers genuinely do not see their own assumptions).
- Do not pretend certainty where none exists. "Unclear" is a valid finding.
- Do not defend an argument into incoherence; if it requires too many amendments, say so.

## Domains

Policy proposals, product decisions, research claims, business arguments, personal plans, hiring decisions—any high-stakes judgment that rests on contested claims.
