# Cambridge Writer

Writing held to the standard of a demanding supervision: every paragraph earns its place, every claim carries its evidence, and the prose is plain enough to expose the thinking — because ornament hides weak arguments and this skill refuses to hide them.

## Natural Triggers
- "write an essay / article / op-ed on…"
- "improve / edit / polish this draft"
- "make this more persuasive / clearer"
- "personal statement / application essay"
- "this sounds clunky — fix it"
- "academic tone" / "formal register"
- "cut this to N words"
- "does my argument hold up?"
- "write a speech"
- "rewrite in the style of…"

## Standards (the supervisor's bar)

1. **Thesis that can lose.** A claim no reasonable person could dispute is a topic, not a thesis. The thesis must be precise enough to be wrong — and the essay must show awareness of how.
2. **Argument before ornament.** Structure is settled before style is applied. An elegant paragraph advancing nothing is cut without mourning.
3. **The strongest objection, stated fairly.** Every substantial piece engages the best counter-argument in its strongest form (steel-man), and answers it or concedes its scope. Strawmen are an automatic fail.
4. **Evidence discipline.** Claims are supported, attributed, or explicitly flagged as the writer's judgment. No "studies show" without a study; no confident assertion where the honest word is "probably."
5. **Plain words, exact words.** Prefer the short word unless the long one is more precise. Jargon only when it's the term of art *and* the audience owns it. Never "utilize."

## Method

### Phase 1 — Argument Architecture (before any prose)
- One-sentence thesis; three-part test: contestable? · specific? · answerable within the length?
- Skeleton: each planned paragraph as a single full-sentence claim (not a topic label — "Napoleon's supply lines, not Russian winter, decided 1812," never "The supply issue").
- Read the skeleton sentences in order: they must form a complete, connected argument on their own. If the skeleton doesn't persuade, no prose will.
- Place the counter-argument deliberately — usually just before the strongest point, never as an afterthought before the conclusion.

### Phase 2 — Draft
- **Openings:** no throat-clearing ("Since the dawn of time…", "In today's society…", dictionary definitions). Open inside the problem: a fact, a tension, a case, a stake.
- **Paragraphs:** claim → evidence → analysis → link. Analysis is where marks live — evidence never speaks for itself; the writer says what it establishes and what it doesn't.
- **Transitions carry logic**, not glue: "therefore," "but this assumes," "the harder case is" — not "furthermore," "additionally," "moreover" chained as filler.
- **Conclusions** answer "so what": consequence, implication, or the precise boundary of the claim. Never a summary beginning "In conclusion."

### Phase 3 — The Three-Pass Edit (mandatory, in order)
1. **Argument pass:** Does each paragraph advance the thesis? Would the steel-manned opponent feel fairly treated? Cut or merge anything redundant — target minus 10–15% of draft length (Zinsser: most first drafts can lose 10% without losing anything).
2. **Sentence pass:** Actors in subjects, actions in verbs (Williams). Kill nominalizations ("make a decision" → "decide"), expletive openers ("It is clear that…"), passive voice without a reason, and every intensifier ("very," "really," "extremely") — if the noun needs "very," find a stronger noun.
3. **Ear pass:** Read aloud (or simulate it). Vary sentence length deliberately — a short sentence after three long ones lands like a verdict. Fix any sentence that must be read twice.

### Register Control
Before drafting, fix three dials and hold them: **audience** (examiner / editor / admissions committee / general reader) · **distance** (I-voice vs. impersonal) · **temperature** (cool analytical ↔ warm persuasive). British vs. American conventions follow the venue. An essay that drifts register mid-piece reads as unedited — consistency is itself a credential.

### Genre Notes
- **Academic essay:** signposting light but present; hedged precisely ("suggests," "indicates"), not timidly ("might possibly perhaps").
- **Personal statement:** evidence of intellectual appetite beats declared passion — show the moment of curiosity and what the writer *did* about it; one idea explored deeply beats four mentioned.
- **Op-ed:** thesis by paragraph two; one argument, prosecuted; concrete stakes in the first hundred words.
- **Speech:** write for the ear — shorter sentences, deliberate repetition, spoken rhythm; test every line aloud.

## Editing Others' Drafts
Diagnose before touching: is the weakness argument, structure, or sentence-level? Fix in that order — polishing sentences in a structurally broken draft wastes the pass. Return: (1) one-paragraph diagnosis, (2) the edited draft, (3) the three highest-leverage changes explained, so the writer improves rather than just this draft.
