# Methods and evidence

Use these methods as design guides, not guarantees. Apply only what fits the topic and format. Keep research explanations out of learner-facing notes unless the learner needs them.

## Cognitive load theory

Working memory is limited. Unnecessary information and poorly coordinated explanations can make learning harder. Sequence concepts, explain diagrams beside their labels, and remove decoration. The best amount of guidance depends on prior knowledge.

**Used here:** one main idea per segment; visual and explanation together; short concept sequence; no decorative content.

Source: Sweller, J., van Merriënboer, J. J. G., & Paas, F. (2019). “Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design: 20 Years Later.” *Educational Psychology Review, 31*, 261–292. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5

## Worked examples

Novices often benefit from studying a completed solution before solving a similar problem. Ask learners to explain why a step works. As expertise grows, remove some of the guidance; do not keep supplying full solutions forever.

**Used here:** a full worked procedure before guided practice; a self-explanation prompt; fewer worked steps for learners with relevant experience.

Source: Sweller, J., & Cooper, G. A. (1985). “The Use of Worked Examples as a Substitute for Problem Solving in Learning Algebra.” *Cognition and Instruction, 2*(1), 59–89. https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3

## Multimedia learning and contiguity

Relevant words and graphics can support learning when designed together. The value comes from the relationship between them, not from adding pictures for their own sake. Multimedia principles include coherence, signaling, pre-training, segmenting, and spatial contiguity.

**Used here:** show a meaningful diagram next to the explanation; label its main relationship; define essential terms first; remove irrelevant visuals.

Source: Mayer, R. E. (2020). *Multimedia Learning* (3rd ed.). Cambridge University Press. https://www.cambridge.org/highereducation/books/multimedia-learning/FB7E79A165D24D47CEACEB4D2C426ECD

## Dual coding

Dual coding theory describes connected verbal and nonverbal representations. A useful diagram can give a learner another route for understanding and recall. This does not mean every picture helps, or that images always outperform words.

**Used here:** pair the explanation with a relevant diagram, table, model, or spatial layout.

Source: Clark, J. M., & Paivio, A. (1991). “Dual Coding Theory and Education.” *Educational Psychology Review, 3*, 149–210. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01320076

## Adult learning and andragogy

Knowles' framework asks educators to consider purpose, learners' experience, readiness, practical problems, and learner agency. These are useful planning questions, not universal laws about every adult.

**Used here:** state why the topic matters; connect concepts to real tasks; respect existing experience without assuming prior subject knowledge.

Source: Knowles, M. S., Holton, E. F., Swanson, R. A., & Robinson, P. A. (2020). *The Adult Learner* (9th ed.). Routledge. https://www.routledge.com/The-Adult-Learner-The-Definitive-Classic-in-Adult-Education-and-Human-Resource-Development/Knowles-Holton-Swanson-Robinson/p/book/9780367417659

## Retrieval practice and spacing

Recalling information can improve later retention. Spreading practice across time can also help. The effect depends on timing, task, feedback, and learner knowledge; questions should support learning rather than punish errors.

**Used here:** a short question after each concept, answers supplied for feedback, and an optional later review plan.

Sources:

- Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). “Test-Enhanced Learning: Taking Memory Tests Improves Long-Term Retention.” *Psychological Science, 17*(3), 249–255. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x
- Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). “Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques.” *Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14*(1), 4–58. https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266

## Self-explanation, contrast, and transfer

Prompts such as “Why does this step work?” can encourage learners to connect a procedure with its reason. Comparing examples and non-examples can expose distinctions. A transfer question asks learners to use the idea in a new context. Choose these moves when they fit; do not add them to every segment by rote.

Evidence note: Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated practice testing and distributed practice highly. They rated self-explanation and interleaving as promising but supported by more limited evidence across contexts. Avoid describing all learning techniques as equally established.

## Boundaries

- No single method guarantees learning for every person or topic.
- A visual can mislead if it hides exceptions or causal complexity.
- Worked examples help many novices, but guidance should fade with expertise.
- Andragogy is a planning framework, not proof that all adults learn alike.
- Retrieval checks need answers and useful feedback.
- These notes are not an assessment of competence.
