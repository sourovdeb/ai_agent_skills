# Established Methods — Grounding for the Lecture Notes Builder

Each method below is a well-established framework in instructional research. The canonical sources are listed so claims can be checked. Boundary conditions are included: these methods support learning for the stated audiences; they are not guarantees.

## 1. Cognitive Load Theory (John Sweller)

Claim: Working memory is limited; instruction must manage load. Three loads are distinguished: intrinsic (the topic's inherent complexity), extraneous (load imposed by poor presentation), and germane (effort building durable knowledge). Reduce extraneous load; manage intrinsic load by sequencing; support schema building. (Sweller, 1988; Sweller, van Merriënboer and Paas, 2019.)

What it forces in the notes: segmentation, simple-to-complex ordering, no split attention, no decorative extras.

## 2. Worked-Example Effect (Sweller; Renkl)

Claim: Novices learn better from studying fully worked examples than from unguided problem solving, because search is eliminated and attention goes to the solution structure. The effect is strongest for novices and fades with expertise (expertise reversal effect); examples work best with self-explanation prompts and as faded sequences. (Sweller and Cooper, 1985; Renkl, 2014; Kirschner, Sweller and Clark, 2006.)

What it forces in the notes: every procedure shown fully worked, step-numbered, before any "you try" task; self-explanation prompts ("why did step 2 work?"); later examples fade steps only after the first fully worked one.

## 3. Split-Attention Effect (Chandler and Sweller)

Claim: When the learner must hold two sources apart (e.g. a diagram and its explanation elsewhere), extraneous load rises. Integrating the text with the diagram improves learning. (Chandler and Sweller, 1991, 1992.)

What it forces in the notes: every visual sits immediately beside the sentence it explains; no separate "figures" section.

## 4. Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (Richard Mayer)

Claim: People process words and pictures through two channels, both limited; learning is better when both channels are used well. Mayer's design principles derived from decades of experiments include: multimedia (words + relevant pictures beat words alone), coherence (cut interesting-but-irrelevant material), signaling (highlight structure), redundancy (avoid repeating the same content in unnecessary parallel forms), spatial contiguity (words near pictures), segmenting (learner-paced small segments), pre-training (know names and characteristics of key concepts first), and personalization (conversational wording). (Mayer, Multimedia Learning, 2001/2021; Mayer, 2023 review in Educational Psychology Review; Mayer and Fiorella, Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning, 2022.)

What it forces in the notes: a visual per concept, adjacency, headings and arrows as signals, a pre-taught key-terms box, short segments, plain conversational language, no decorative images.

Boundary: some principles have documented exceptions (e.g. on-screen text can help non-native speakers or when audio is unavailable); break a principle only with a stated reason, never by default.

## 5. Dual Coding Theory (Allan Paivio)

Claim: Verbal and nonverbal information are processed as distinct but connected systems; material coded both ways is more memorable than material coded once (picture superiority, concreteness effects). Concreteness and imagery aid recall and comprehension. (Paivio, 1986; Clark and Paivio, 1991.)

What it forces in the notes: every abstract concept first arrives as a concrete visual or analogy, then gets its formal wording. Concrete examples before formal definitions.

## 6. Andragogy — Adult Learning (Malcolm Knowles)

Claim: Six assumptions distinguish adult learners: (1) adults need to know why before committing; (2) adults carry experience that learning should build on; (3) adults need to direct their own learning; (4) adults are ready to learn what solves an immediate problem; (5) adults orient to problems and performance, not subjects; (6) adults respond more to internal than external motivators. (Knowles, The Adult Learner; Knowles, 1984.)

What it forces in the notes: a why-it-matters opening tied to the audience's real context, workplace or daily-life examples, problem-centered segments, and a promise the learner can check. These are design assumptions, not laws about every adult; use them as a checklist.

## 7. Retrieval Practice / Testing Effect (Roediger and Karpicke)

Claim: Actively retrieving information strengthens memory more than re-reading. Small, low-stakes checks with feedback are an established study method. (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006.)

What it forces in the notes: a question with its answer after each segment, and a five-question quick review with answers at the end.

## Boundary conditions and honesty

- The worked-example effect reverses for experts; these notes target novices, so the novice rule stands.
- Multimedia principles carry documented exceptions; each departure must state its reason.
- Andragogy's six assumptions are design principles, not empirical laws about adults.
- Following established methods raises the design quality of the notes; it does not establish that any particular reader learned. Never claim validated effectiveness, classroom testing, or expert review that did not happen.
