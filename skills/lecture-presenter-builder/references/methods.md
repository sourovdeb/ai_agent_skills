# Method grounding and boundaries

Apply methods to improve the lecture; do not recite theory names to the audience. No single design guarantees learning.

## Cognitive load and worked examples

Manage the number of new ideas at once. Sequence the explanation and keep labels beside the diagram they explain. For a new procedure, show a completed example and its reasoning before practice. Reduce guidance as prior knowledge grows.

**Grounding:** Sweller, J., van Merriënboer, J. J. G., & Paas, F. (2019). “Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design: 20 Years Later.” *Educational Psychology Review, 31*, 261–292. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5

## Multimedia learning

Use relevant words and graphics together. Remove material that does not serve the teaching goal. Signal structure clearly; align labels with the part they explain; reveal complex systems in segments. Do not mechanically ban text: it may be useful for accessibility, technical terms, or audiences who cannot hear narration.

**Grounding:** Mayer, R. E. *Multimedia Learning* (3rd ed.). Cambridge University Press, 2020. https://www.cambridge.org/highereducation/books/multimedia-learning/FB7E79A165D24D47CEACEB4D2C426ECD

## Dual coding and visual explanation

A diagram or concrete visual can complement verbal explanation. It must show the relevant relationship. Images do not automatically improve learning; irrelevant or ambiguous visuals can distract.

**Grounding:** Clark, J. M., & Paivio, A. (1991). “Dual Coding Theory and Education.” *Educational Psychology Review, 3*, 149–210. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01320076

## Adult learning

Adults often benefit from knowing the purpose and from seeing how an idea applies to an actual problem. Respect work and life experience without assuming prior subject knowledge. Treat these as planning heuristics, not claims about all adults.

**Grounding:** Knowles, M. S., Holton, E. F., Swanson, R. A., & Robinson, P. A. (2020). *The Adult Learner* (9th ed.). Routledge. https://www.routledge.com/The-Adult-Learner-The-Definitive-Classic-in-Adult-Education-and-Human-Resource-Development/Knowles-Holton-Swanson-Robinson/p/book/9780367417659

## Retrieval and spaced practice

Ask learners to retrieve an idea before showing the answer. Give feedback. When relevant, set a later retrieval task rather than relying on a final summary alone. Retrieval should be low-stakes and related to the lesson's outcome.

**Grounding:** Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). “Test-Enhanced Learning: Taking Memory Tests Improves Long-Term Retention.” *Psychological Science, 17*(3), 249–255. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x

**Broad review:** Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). “Improving Students’ Learning With Effective Learning Techniques.” *Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14*(1), 4–58. https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266

## Human voice and story

Use a concrete case, a question, or a decision to give the idea stakes. Keep the story proportional to the teaching goal. Do not fabricate a source-backed anecdote. Narrative is a delivery choice, not a substitute for evidence.

## Boundaries

- Research quality depends on the topic and the population studied.
- Analogies are partial models; state where they stop fitting.
- Visual layouts can imply causality or certainty that the evidence does not support.
- Retrieval and worked examples should fit the audience's prior knowledge.
- A coherent script is not proof of learning or competence.
