# Red-Flag Triage Reference

Concrete thresholds drawn from major clinical/public-health bodies (AHA, CDC, WHO, NIA, SAMHSA/988, AAP). Use this to decide whether a conversation needs to stop and redirect to emergency care before continuing with general education. When in doubt, escalate — these thresholds are intentionally conservative.

## Cardiac (source: American Heart Association)
Escalate to **call emergency services now, do not self-drive** if the person describes:
- Chest pressure, squeezing, fullness, or pain lasting more than a few minutes, or that goes away and comes back.
- Pain or discomfort spreading to one or both arms, the back, neck, jaw, or stomach.
- Shortness of breath, with or without chest discomfort.
- Cold sweat, nausea, or lightheadedness accompanying any of the above.
- Note women, older adults, and people with diabetes more often present atypically (fatigue, jaw/back pain, nausea, without classic chest pain) — don't rule out cardiac cause just because the presentation isn't textbook.

## Stroke — BE-FAST (source: CDC / AHA)
Escalate immediately — "brain cells die every minute untreated" — if any of:
- **B**alance: sudden loss of balance or coordination.
- **E**yes: sudden vision loss or double vision.
- **F**ace: facial drooping, especially one-sided.
- **A**rm: one arm drifts down or feels weak when both are raised.
- **S**peech: slurred, garbled, or unusually hard to produce.
- **T**ime: note when symptoms started (needed for treatment eligibility) and call emergency services immediately.

## Sepsis (source: CDC)
Escalate to the ED now if two or more of the following appear together, especially after a known infection, surgery, or wound:
- Temperature above 100.4°F (38°C) or below 96.8°F (36°C).
- Heart rate above 90 bpm.
- Respiratory rate above 20 breaths/min.
- Altered mental status (confusion, unusual sleepiness, difficulty rousing).
A useful phrase to suggest the person use with a clinician or ED intake: "I'm concerned this could be sepsis."

## Anaphylaxis
- If someone has a known severe allergy and develops hives plus swelling of lips/face/throat, difficulty breathing, wheezing, or dizziness/fainting after a likely exposure: use epinephrine auto-injector immediately if available, then call emergency services — even if symptoms seem to improve after the injection, they still need emergency evaluation unless a clinician has told them otherwise for their specific case.

## Mental health crisis (source: 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, SAMHSA)
- 988 (call, text, or chat, in the US) is for any crisis or significant emotional distress, not only active suicidal intent — encourage reaching out well before a crisis point is reached.
- Distinguish passive thoughts of death/not wanting to exist from active risk: active risk means a specific plan, access to means, and a timeline, or a prior attempt. That combination, or an attempt in progress, is when to involve emergency services (call 911 / local emergency number) rather than only the crisis line.
- Never leave a disclosure of active suicidal or homicidal intent unaddressed to "get back to the original question" — pause and address it directly, with warmth, before anything else.

## Pediatric-specific
- Fever of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in an infant under 3 months old warrants immediate medical evaluation regardless of how the baby otherwise looks or acts — young infants can be seriously ill without looking it.
- At any pediatric age: trouble breathing, a seizure, unresponsiveness, or a rash that doesn't fade when pressed (possible meningococcal) means call emergency services now.
- Dehydration signs in a child (no tears when crying, no wet diaper in 8+ hours, sunken eyes, lethargy) warrant same-day medical attention.

## Obstetric-specific
- Heavy vaginal bleeding, severe abdominal pain, severe headache with visual changes, or reduced fetal movement in pregnancy: same-day or emergency evaluation, don't wait for a scheduled appointment.

## Older-adult-specific
- A sudden change in mental status, mobility, or continence in an older adult is often the only sign of a serious underlying problem (infection, stroke, medication reaction) and should not be dismissed as "just aging" — recommend same-day evaluation.
- New falls, especially with head impact or on blood thinners, warrant medical evaluation even without obvious injury.

## When it's genuinely not urgent
Most of what people ask about — a cold, a minor ache, a stable chronic-condition question, a general "is this normal" question — is not on this list. Say so plainly; false urgency is also a disservice. The point of this reference is to make sure the small set of situations that truly can't wait get caught immediately, not to make every conversation feel like an emergency.
