# Family Memory Deep Probe with Reliability Coding

This is the most careful variant in the biography-interview family, built for two related but distinct situations: interviewing someone whose memory itself may be unreliable, and reconciling accounts that conflict across multiple interviewees. Both require treating reliability as a first-class part of the output, not an afterthought.

Read `../biography-memoir-system/references/interview-methodology.md` in full before running a session — the "Reliability coding" and "Working with older or memory-impaired narrators" sections are core to this variant, not optional background.

## When the narrator has memory impairment or dementia

- **Lead with validation, not correction.** If something doesn't match known fact, don't challenge it in the moment — the emotional content usually matters more than precision here, and correcting can cause real distress or shut the conversation down entirely. Tag the discrepancy privately for later instead.
- **Keep sessions short and match their best time of day.** 20-30 minutes at a consistent, well-rested time beats a longer session that runs into fatigue — fatigue tends to produce reconstruction that sounds like memory but isn't, which should be tagged accordingly if it happens.
- **Use concrete objects, not abstract prompts.** A photograph, a letter, a piece of music, a familiar object reliably outperforms "tell me about your life" as a way into real recall.
- **Notice when something is beyond this interview's scope.** New or worsening confusion, real distress, or signs the family hasn't openly discussed a dementia diagnosis are signals to pause the interview and gently point the family toward the person's clinician or a resource like the Alzheimer's Association — this skill captures memory, it doesn't assess or manage cognitive health, and conflating the two does a disservice to both goals.

## When reconciling conflicting family accounts

- **Interview each relative independently first**, without relaying what others have said — cross-contamination between accounts (someone adjusting their memory to match what they just heard a sibling say) is one of the most common ways a family record quietly loses accuracy.
- **Hold every account with its own reliability tag** (directly witnessed / told to them / family legend / reconstructed / uncertain) rather than resolving the conflict by picking whichever version came from the "more reliable" relative — reliability tagging is about the nature of each specific claim, not a blanket ranking of people.
- **Present conflicts as conflicts in the output**, not as a single smoothed narrative — "Aunt Rosa remembers the move happening the year before the wedding; Uncle Tomás remembers it as the year after" is more honest and often more interesting than silently picking one. Where it matters and evidence exists, a light corroboration pass against records (see `skills/content-research-writer`) can help, but plenty of family conflicts are genuinely unresolvable and should be presented that way.
- **Watch for the family dynamics underneath a factual disagreement.** Sometimes a persistent disagreement about "what really happened" is really about something else (old sibling rivalry, differing loyalties) — you don't need to resolve that, but naming it gently can help the family understand why the accounts diverge, rather than treating it as a pure memory-accuracy problem.

## Output

A tagged record — per the shared methodology's reliability categories — that makes clear, for every claim of real significance, how confident the family should be in it and why. This is the variant where the reliability tags are the deliverable, not a background annotation: a reader should be able to tell at a glance what's solid, what's family legend, and what's still disputed.
