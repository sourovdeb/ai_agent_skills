# Core Interview Methodology

Shared technique reference for the biography/memoir skill family (`biography-interview-kit-private-master`, `biography-interview-kit-public`, `dual-biography-interview-skill-v2`, `memory-elicitation-interview-skill`, `family-memory-deep-probe-reliability`). Each of those skills points here for the underlying method rather than repeating it — read this once, then read the specific skill's SKILL.md for what's distinct about that variant.

## Eliciting real memories, not rehearsed anecdotes

Most people, asked "tell me about your childhood," produce the three stories they've told at dinner parties for twenty years. Getting past the rehearsed version to something with real texture requires specific technique, not just more time:

- **Cue with the senses, not the summary.** "What did the kitchen smell like on a Sunday?" surfaces different, less-rehearsed material than "what was your childhood like?" Sensory and spatial cues (a room, a smell, a walk to school) reliably unlock adjacent memories that a topical question doesn't.
- **Anchor to a specific moment, not a period.** "Tell me about a time your father was angry" beats "what was your father like?" — a specific incident forces concrete recall instead of a summarized trait.
- **Build a timeline as scaffolding, out loud.** Establishing "so you were around 8 when you moved to that house" gives the narrator a peg to hang subsequent memories on, and often triggers recall of what else was happening then that they hadn't thought to mention.
- **Follow the flicker, not just the plan.** If a prepared question gets a flat answer but a throwaway aside gets a change in energy — a pause, a laugh, a shift in tone — follow the aside. That's usually where the real material is.
- **Ask what it felt like, separately from what happened.** People narrate events readily but skip the feeling unless asked directly and separately: "what happened" then, distinctly, "what did that feel like at the time" and "how do you feel about it now" — those can be three different answers.
- **Let silence do work.** Resist filling a pause with the next question. A few extra seconds of silence after an answer often produces an unprompted addition that a follow-up question would have preempted.

## Reliability coding

Not every account carries the same evidentiary weight, and a good interview kit says so rather than flattening everything into equally-confident prose. Tag material as you go (lightly, not as a bureaucratic overlay) using something like:

- **Directly witnessed** — the narrator was there and describing their own direct experience.
- **Told to them** — secondhand, from a specific named source ("my mother always said...").
- **Family legend** — repeated within the family with no clear original source, possibly embellished over generations.
- **Reconstructed / inferred** — the narrator is piecing together what "must have" happened from context, not recalling it directly.
- **Uncertain / conflicting** — the narrator flags their own doubt, or two accounts from different people conflict.

This isn't about interrogating a grandmother's honesty — it's about giving the eventual written memoir a way to say "as she remembered it" versus "as the family always told it" without either overclaiming precision or undermining the story's value. Family legend and reconstructed memory are still worth capturing; they're just a different kind of true.

## Working with older or memory-impaired narrators

When the narrator is elderly, has some cognitive decline, or has a diagnosed dementia, standard interview technique needs real adaptation, not just a gentler tone:

- **Follow validation-therapy principles**: if a detail is inaccurate or contradicts known fact, don't correct it mid-interview — the emotional truth of what they're conveying usually matters more than the factual precision, and correction can cause distress or shut the conversation down. Note the discrepancy privately for reliability coding rather than challenging it aloud.
- **Short sessions, familiar time of day.** Cognitive stamina for this kind of recall work is often much shorter than it looks from the outside — 20-30 minutes at a consistent, well-rested time of day beats a long session that ends in fatigue and frustration.
- **Concrete objects as memory triggers** work better than abstract questions: photographs, an old letter, a piece of music, a familiar object — these anchor recall far more reliably than "tell me about the 1960s."
- **Expect and welcome repetition and non-linearity.** The same story told three ways in one session isn't a failure of the interview — each telling can surface a slightly different detail, and stitching them together afterward (in the reliability-coding pass) is part of the work, not a sign to redirect impatiently.
- **Watch for signs that go beyond an interview's scope**: real distress, confusion that seems new or worsening, or signs the family hasn't discussed a dementia diagnosis openly. When that comes up, gently pause and suggest the family bring it to the person's clinician or a resource like the Alzheimer's Association — an interview session is not the place to assess or manage a cognitive health concern, only to notice when one may exist.

## Navigating sensitive material

Biography and memoir work regularly surfaces grief, estrangement, abuse, addiction, or shame. None of that is a reason to steer away from real material, but it does change how you ask:

- Ask permission before going somewhere clearly painful: "There's a part of this I think matters to the story, but I want to check it's okay to ask about — how your parents' divorce affected you at the time?" Consent asked explicitly, rather than assumed, changes how safe the conversation feels.
- If someone becomes distressed, shift into listening mode (see the `empathetic-listening` skill for concrete technique) before returning to information-gathering, or drop the thread entirely if they signal they'd rather not continue.
- Never publish or share material the narrator hasn't explicitly agreed should be shared at that level of detail — see `biography-interview-kit-public` for how the private/public boundary is actually enforced, not just assumed.
