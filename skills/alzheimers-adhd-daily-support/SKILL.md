# Alzheimer's / Dementia and ADHD Daily Support

These are two different populations with one thing in common: generic advice ("just make a to-do list," "just remind them") doesn't work, because the underlying limitation is neurological, not a matter of trying harder. Give technique specific enough to actually change what happens in someone's day.

## Part 1 — Supporting someone with Alzheimer's or dementia

### The single most important communication shift: stop correcting reality

The instinct to gently correct a factual error ("Mom, Dad passed away, remember?") feels honest, but for someone with dementia it can mean re-experiencing the loss for the first time, over and over, with no memory of having grieved it before. This is the core insight of **validation therapy** (Naomi Feil): address the *emotion* behind what someone is saying or asking for, not the *factual accuracy* of it.

- If they ask for a parent who died years ago, respond to the underlying feeling ("You miss her. Tell me about her.") rather than correcting the fact.
- If they insist on an inaccurate date, name, or event, don't argue the point — agree enough to move forward, then redirect to another topic or activity.
- Reserve factual correction ("reality orientation") for early-stage, mild disorientation where it genuinely helps rather than distresses — it's not a blanket rule, and it stops being appropriate as the disease progresses. When unsure, err toward validating the feeling.

### Environment and routine

- Fix daily times for bathing, dressing, eating, and sleep — predictability reduces agitation more than almost any single other intervention.
- Schedule the hardest tasks (bathing, appointments) during the person's best time of day, usually earlier, to reduce resistance.
- Use environmental cues instead of relying on memory: picture labels on doors, color-coded hot/cold faucet markers, nightlights or motion-activated lights along paths to the bathroom, higher-contrast lighter walls against darker floors so edges are easier to see, and eye-level decals on glass doors to prevent walking into them.

### Safety: wandering and medication

- Wandering is common and dangerous — recommend a wearable ID (e.g., MedicAlert-style engraved ID) linked to a 24/7 emergency response service that can trigger a missing-person alert network, plus door alarms or sensors if wandering has already occurred.
- Medication management (what to take, when, dose changes) is not something to advise on directly — that's the prescribing clinician's or pharmacist's call. What you can help with: building an external system (pill organizer, alarm-linked reminders, a written schedule posted where it's used) so the *administration* is easier to keep consistent.

### The caregiver matters too

Caregiver burnout is extremely common and directly affects the quality of care given. Proactively mention, don't wait to be asked:
- Respite care (even a few hours a week) is not indulgent, it's protective of the relationship long-term.
- Peer support exists specifically for this — e.g., the Alzheimer's Association's 24/7 Helpline and ALZConnected community — and caregivers who use it report less isolation and better coping.
- If a caregiver describes exhaustion, resentment, or guilt, validate that these are normal responses to a genuinely hard, ongoing loss — not a moral failing.

## Part 2 — Supporting someone with ADHD

### The core reframe: it's not a motivation problem, it's an externalization problem

Russell Barkley's model of ADHD centers on a deficit in self-regulation and "time blindness" — the future is abstract and doesn't pull behavior the way it does for a neurotypical brain. The fix isn't more willpower; it's moving information and time out of the head and into the environment, at the point where it's needed.

- **Externalize time.** Analog clocks and visual timers (a timer with a visibly shrinking colored disk, not just a digital countdown) make time concrete instead of abstract. Stack alarms at 30/15/0 minutes before a transition rather than one single reminder — a single alarm is easy to dismiss and forget instantly.
- **Externalize memory at the point of performance.** A single planner that lives in a bag is often useless — the reminder needs to be physically present where and when the action has to happen (a note taped to the door for what to bring, a checklist by the coffee maker for morning meds). Duplicate the same reminder in multiple physical locations rather than trusting one central system.
- **Break tasks into physically written steps.** "Clean the kitchen" is one invisible blob of a task that's easy to freeze in front of; "1) clear counters 2) load dishwasher 3) wipe counters 4) take out trash" is four small, startable actions.

### Getting unstuck on task initiation

- **The 2-minute rule as a starting trick** (distinct from its GTD use): commit to just 2 minutes of a dreaded task, no more — momentum after starting is often the actual barrier, not the task itself.
- **Temptation bundling**: pair a task that's hard to start with something enjoyable that only happens during it (a favorite podcast only while doing paperwork).
- **Body doubling**: working alongside another person — in person or over video (services like Focusmate exist specifically for this) — without necessarily sharing the same task. The external presence substitutes for the internal activation that's harder to generate alone.
- **The "Wall of Awful"** (Brendan Mahan's term): the pileup of shame and avoidance that builds around a task the longer it's avoided, which makes it feel even harder to start than the task itself actually is. Look for an easier entry point using **PINCH** — Play, Interest, Novelty, Challenge — some angle on the task that's more engaging than the plain version, rather than pushing straight at the wall.

### What to avoid

- Don't suggest "just try harder to remember" or generic planner advice as if the problem is a lack of a system — the person likely has tried systems that require sustained internal tracking, and those are exactly what doesn't work here.
- Don't frame time-blindness or forgetfulness as a character flaw ("you're just lazy/careless") — reflecting that reframe back explicitly ("this isn't a willpower issue, it's how your attention system works") is often itself useful support.
- Medication is a legitimate and often highly effective part of ADHD treatment — but as with dementia, dosing and medication decisions route to the prescribing clinician, not to advice given here.
